# 17 · Writing: how anything you send should sound

This file is the only owner of how your writing sounds, on every surface: résumés, cover letters,
form answers, messages to founders, recruiters and hiring managers, emails, replies, follow-ups,
thank-you notes, LinkedIn posts, profile and bio copy, connection notes and DMs, cold outreach,
portfolio or work-sample copy if you keep one, interview talking points, and the replies an agent
writes to you in chat. Every other file links here and never restates a voice rule.

A long list of banned shapes teaches a writer to write to the checker: the copy passes and still
reads like a machine. The only test that holds up is reading it aloud as yourself, and a second
reader doing the same. The two scripts this file mentions are a floor under that test, never a
replacement for it.

## The one test

Read it out loud as yourself, to this one person. Would you say it that way to someone you have known
for years? If a sentence sounds like a brochure, a résumé bullet, a press release or a machine,
rewrite it until it sounds like you talking.

## Why generated applications read as generated

1. **Nothing real to stand on.** A writer given only a job post and a fact sheet has no story, reason
   or opinion of yours to use, so it fills the gap with the average applicant: a moment of being
   drawn in, an insight about the employer's business, a feeling.
2. **Instructions that ask for invention.** "Say why this company excites you" can only be answered
   by making something up if you never said why.
3. **Bridges instead of talk.** Quote the posting, then tie it to a line from the résumé. It's a
   template, and a reader sees that in one line.
4. **A reviewer with the same blind spot.** A reviewer that has never seen your own writing passes
   whatever sounds competent. Give it your own text to compare against.

## Where the words come from (the main rule)

- **Your material, in your words.** Build every cover letter and every "why us" or "about you" answer
  from one or two entries in `knowledge-base/18-in-your-own-words.md`, retold shorter, the way you
  told them. `03-career-narrative.md` and your voice samples below count too.
- **Facts** about you come from `knowledge-base/`. Facts about the employer come from a stored source
  in the dossier's `sources/`.
- **Never invent an inner life.** No moment of discovery, feeling, reaction, motive, opinion or habit
  that isn't in your material or your own words. If nothing fits this employer, say less: a few true
  sentences do more than a longer paragraph someone made up. A blank file 18 is fine; the letter is just short.
- **Cut, don't hedge.** A claim that needs a qualifier to stay true gets cut.
- **Never copy a sentence** from this file, the knowledge base, a dossier or another letter. Wording
  that gets reused turns into a template fast.

## What good writing means here (the general principles)

These hold for anyone. Your own description above sharpens them; where the two disagree, yours wins.

1. **Plain, everyday words.** The words you'd use across a table, not the words of a job post.
2. **Full sentences that carry their reason.** Join thoughts the way people talk ("so", "because",
   "which is why"). A run of short, clipped sentences reads like a list of bold claims. A short
   sign-off line is fine. If you naturally write short, your samples win.
3. **Write like you have known them for years.** Relaxed and direct, "you" and "your team". No
   ceremony, no "I am writing to express my interest".
4. **Leave nothing ambiguous.** Say who did what and why. The first time a term the reader may not
   know comes up (an internal product name, an acronym from your field), explain it in plain words in
   that sentence, then keep using plain words instead of repeating the acronym. Don't squeeze facts
   into side clauses; give them their own sentence.
5. **An example when it helps something click.** A well-known product or an everyday situation. It
   has to be accurate. One or two per message at most.
6. **A little dry humor is welcome if it's yours**, about the situation, never at the reader or
   anyone's company.
7. **Numbers, calmly.** Put the number in a normal sentence with what it measured. No trophy verbs
   (drove, boosted, achieved, delivered, transformed), and no cause your records don't support.
8. **Tie your work to theirs in one plain sentence, if you have something real.** Check their live
   site the day you write, because companies change. Say a plain fact about what their customers,
   patients, students or crews do, in your words, and what you'd want to do there. Never hand their
   posting back to them, recite their website, explain their business to them, or weld one of their
   numbers to your reason. Don't tell them their work is just like your old job; said once it's fine,
   said by habit it becomes over-confidence.
9. **Never over-confident.** No "that's exactly how I work", "the same problem I solved", "I know
   exactly how to", "perfect fit", nothing that sounds like you have it all figured out. No selling,
   bragging or trying to prove anything. And no apologizing or naming gaps either: plain, true things
   about your work are enough.
10. **Open and close like your own emails.** Use the greeting and sign-off you actually use (see your
    section above). End on the next step or an easy line.
11. **Punctuation.** Periods and commas. No em-dashes in anything you send (`scripts/voice_check.py`
    flags them). A colon only where people normally use one, not to set up a reveal.
12. **Every message is written fresh, even in a batch.** When several go out the same day, no two
    share an opening, an introduction of you, a closing, a joke or an analogy, and each picks the one
    piece of your work that fits that company instead of the same list every time. The reviewer reads
    a batch side by side to catch this.
13. **No line or question just for the sake of it.** Ask only what you actually need answered, and
    cut any sentence that is there to fill space or sound polite. `voice_check.py` flags the common
    filler questions; if you really do want the answer, keep it.
14. **Thank someone once.** In a follow-up or a reply in a thread where you already thanked them,
    don't thank them again, and don't repeat an offer you already made.

## What a human application sounds like

- It opens plainly: which job, and the one reason or story. Nobody needs a clever first line.
- It has one small, true, concrete thing in it: something a customer said, a place you went, a
  decision you made, a number. Real details are a little messy and don't wrap up neatly.
- It says what you did and what happened, not what kind of worker you are.
- It ends short: thanks, or the next step. No summary of why you fit.
- **The swap test.** If another employer's name could go in and the sentence still works, it says
  nothing about them. If any applicant could have written it, it says nothing about you.

**Telling a story.** Give the context first, in a sentence or two: where you were, what you did there,
and what that place or tool is in plain words. Then tell it in the order it happened: how it started,
what happened, what you did, how it went. Don't start in the middle, and end on how it went, not on
what it proves. Describe people the way you'd say them out loud, not by their job title. Describe a
tool or process by what it's for from the point of view of the person using it, never as a list of its
functions.

**Choosing and ending.**

- Pick the story for this employer. Don't default to your best-known one.
- One story per letter, not a tour of your career. The résumé is attached.
- A "why us" answer opens from your real reason, in two or three sentences. Don't open by telling
  them what they are. `knowledge-base/12-application-answers.md` owns what goes in it.
- End on things that belong together, worded fresh each time, such as where you'd work and where to
  see your work if you have some to show. Mention a start date only if they asked.

## The same voice on surfaces that aren't messages

- **Résumé.** Each line is a plain sentence or phrase a person could read aloud, not a clipped claim:
  what you did, for whom, and what came of it, in everyday words. No trophy verbs, no titles you
  didn't hold, no stacked numbers; a number sits in the line with what it measured. Whether lines are
  first person or implied-subject is your call; record it in your rules below.
- **Cover letter.** Which job, one real story that fits what they do, one plain sentence on them,
  where to see your work if you have some to show, thanks.
- **LinkedIn, connection notes, DMs, cold outreach.** Short because the box is short, never clipped:
  one or two full sentences that say why you're writing to this person, tied to something real about
  their work, and an easy ask. A first cold message is about 60 to 90 words; a link to your work is
  optional. (An agent drafts these; you send them by hand.)
- **A first reply to someone who reached out.** The same limit, and no work story: yes to the role
  (or what you'd want to know first), where your résumé or work is, what's needed next. The stories
  wait until they ask.
- **Replies, follow-ups and thank-you notes.** Like your own sent emails: answer what they asked, end
  on the next step.
- **A notes box on a booking or scheduling form.** One short ordinary line, no agenda, no pitch.
  "Thanks, see you then." is plenty.
- **Form answers.** Answer the question that's actually printed, in plain sentences.
  Work-authorization questions are answered from `knowledge-base/12-application-answers.md`, and only
  where asked.
- **Chat replies to you.** Plain words, full sentences, lead with the answer, no jargon or labels
  you'd have to decode.

## How a piece of writing gets made

1. Read the job post and the company's live site today; store what you use in the dossier's
   `sources/` with its date (CLAUDE.md §0.1).
2. Pick one or two entries from `18-in-your-own-words.md` that fit what they do, and the facts that
   go with them. If nothing fits, plan a shorter piece.
3. Write it in your voice, the way you'd say it on a call, using the sections above.
4. Read it out loud as yourself and fix what doesn't sound like you.
5. **A fresh reviewer reads it**: a reviewer agent that sees only this file, your voice samples and
   reference letter, your file 18, the draft and the facts it cites, and nothing of the conversation
   that produced it. It marks every sentence that sounds machine-made, over-confident, salesy, unclear
   or untrue. Fix those by saying the real thing plainer or cutting it, never by adding qualifiers,
   and review once more if anything changed.
6. `python3 scripts/voice_check.py --sendable <file>` checks only the hard facts and named phrases
   (dashes, the over-confidence phrases above, filler questions, a work-authorization mention nobody
   asked for, and your never-say list). Passing it says nothing about whether the voice is right; only
   steps 4 and 5 can.
7. `python3 scripts/slop_check.py <dossier>` (or `--text`, or `--letter`) points at sentence shapes
   that often read as machine-made. Only two shapes can hold a piece back: handing the employer's
   posting back as your reason, and claiming their way of working is already yours. Everything else
   is a "second look": read that sentence again, and if it's true and it's how you'd say it, leave
   it. Never reword a sentence just to get past the script. Across a batch, `--batch` lists phrases
   three or more letters share.
